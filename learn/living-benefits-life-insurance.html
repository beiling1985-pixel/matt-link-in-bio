What are living benefits on life insurance? Critical, chronic and terminal illness explained

By Matt Beiling, licensed insurance agent. Updated October 27, 2026.

Short answer: Living benefits let some life insurance policies pay part of your coverage while you're still alive, if you're diagnosed with a qualifying critical, chronic or terminal illness. Whatever you take early comes out of the amount your family would get later. You don't pay it back.

Most people think of life insurance as money for your family after you die. That's still the main job. But when I sit down with Ohio homeowners, the question that worries them just as much is: "What happens to the house if I get sick and can't work?" That's where living benefits come in.

What living benefits are

"Living benefits" is the everyday name for what insurance companies usually call accelerated death benefits. They're features, often added as riders, on many term and permanent life insurance policies. If you meet the policy's definition of a serious illness, you can ask the carrier to pay out part of your death benefit early.

You can use that money however you want. Some people keep the mortgage paid while they're out of work. Others cover medical bills, travel for treatment, or help at home. The carrier doesn't tell you how to spend it.

The three types, in plain English

Critical illness

This can pay after a diagnosis of a specific, serious condition named in the policy. Depending on the carrier, the list may include things like certain heart attacks, strokes, cancers, kidney failure or a major organ transplant. Each condition has its own definition in the policy, and the diagnosis has to meet it. Not every cancer or heart problem will qualify, so the exact wording matters.

Chronic illness

This can pay if you can't take care of yourself the way you used to. Most policies use a standard test: you can no longer do two of six basic "activities of daily living" without help, or you have a severe cognitive impairment such as advanced dementia. The six activities are:

  • Bathing
  • Dressing
  • Eating
  • Using the toilet
  • Moving in and out of a bed or chair (called transferring)
  • Continence

Many policies also require that a doctor expects the condition to last a long time or be permanent. Some pay a lump sum, others pay in installments, and many set a yearly limit.

Terminal illness

This can pay if a doctor certifies that you have a limited time to live. The cutoff depends on the policy, often 12 or 24 months. This is the most common type, and many policies include some version of it.

How the money works

Living benefits are not extra insurance stacked on top of your policy. They're an early draw from the same pot of money. Here's what that means in practice:

  • It reduces the death benefit. If you take part of the coverage early, your beneficiaries get the rest, not the full amount.
  • It's usually not a dollar-for-dollar payout. Many carriers pay a discounted amount when you use the benefit, based on things like your age, health and how long they expect you to live. Some also charge a small processing fee.
  • There's usually a limit. Policies cap how much of the coverage you can take early, and the cap can differ by type of illness.
  • You don't repay it. It isn't a loan.

How carriers charge for living benefits varies too. Some build them into the policy with no added upfront cost and make the adjustment only if you use them. Others sell them as a separate rider with its own premium. When you compare policies, ask how each one handles this.

Things to know before you count on them

  • Definitions vary by carrier. Two policies that both say "critical illness" can cover different conditions. I always look at the actual wording, not just the name of the rider.
  • Not every policy has all three. Some include only terminal illness. Others include critical and chronic as well. Availability also depends on your state, age and health.
  • Living benefits aren't long-term care insurance or health insurance. They can help with costs, but they work differently and have different rules.
  • Taxes and government benefits. Accelerated benefits can have tax effects, and receiving a large payment could affect eligibility for need-based programs such as Medicaid. This is general information, not legal or tax advice. Talk to a tax professional about your situation.

Why this matters for homeowners

A mortgage doesn't pause because you're in treatment. If your household depends on two incomes, losing one for months can put the house at risk long before anyone thinks about a death benefit. That's why many mortgage protection plans today include living benefits. The same policy that protects your family if you die can also help keep the payments going if you get seriously ill.

Questions I'd ask about any policy

  • Which of the three types does it include: critical, chronic, terminal?
  • Which conditions count, and how are they defined?
  • What's the most I could take early, and is the payout discounted?
  • Is there an added premium for the rider, or a fee when I use it?
  • Does it pay as a lump sum or in installments?

If you already have a policy, check your paperwork or call the carrier with these same questions. You might have more protection than you realize, or less.
