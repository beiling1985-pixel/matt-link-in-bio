How much life insurance do I need to cover my mortgage?

By Matt Beiling, licensed insurance agent. Updated November 3, 2026.

Short answer: Start with what you still owe on the house today, your payoff balance. Pick a term that lasts at least as long as the years left on your loan. Then add what your family would still need to live there: taxes, insurance, upkeep, other debts and some income. That total is your target.

This is one of the first questions I get from Ohio homeowners. Most people expect a single magic number. There isn't one, but there is a simple way to work it out on the back of an envelope. Here's how I walk people through it.

Step 1: find your payoff balance

Use what you owe now, not what you borrowed. If you've been paying for eight years, your balance is lower than the loan you signed for.

  • Your monthly mortgage statement shows your principal balance.
  • For the exact number, ask your loan servicer for a payoff quote. It includes interest owed up to a certain date, so it's usually a bit higher than the principal on your statement.
  • If you have a home equity loan or line of credit, add that balance too. It's tied to the house.

This number is the floor. Coverage below it would leave your family short of paying off the home.

Step 2: match the term to the years left

Most mortgage protection is term life insurance, which covers you for a set number of years. A common approach is to choose a term at least as long as the time left on your loan. If you have 22 years left, a 20-year term would end two years early, so many people would look at a longer one.

Which terms you can get depends on the carrier, the product, your age and your health. Some terms may not be offered past a certain age.

Step 3: remember the house still costs money after it's paid off

Paying off the loan doesn't make the house free. If your property taxes and homeowners insurance are paid through escrow today, they're hiding inside your mortgage payment. Once the loan is gone, your family pays those bills directly, every year.

Add a cushion for:

  • Property taxes and homeowners insurance
  • Repairs and upkeep, like a roof, furnace or water heater
  • Utilities, which don't drop just because the mortgage did

Step 4: add what your family needs beyond the house

A paid-off home helps a lot, but your family still has to buy groceries and keep the lights on. Think about:

  • Other debts: car loans, credit cards, student loans.
  • Income: how many months or years of your paycheck your family would need to adjust.
  • Kids: childcare now, and any college help you'd want to provide.
  • Final expenses: funeral and burial costs come due quickly.

You don't have to cover every item. Some families only want the house handled. Others want the house plus a few years of breathing room. Both are reasonable. It comes down to what your family would need and what fits your budget.

A simple example

Say a Medina couple owes about $180,000 on their home with 22 years left. They also have a car loan of about $15,000. They want their family to have roughly two years of help with taxes, insurance, upkeep and bills while things settle.

Their worksheet looks like this: the $180,000 payoff, plus the $15,000 car loan, plus whatever two years of those costs adds up to for them. They'd look at a term of at least 22 years. These numbers are made up to show the math. Yours will be different.

Level or decreasing coverage?

Some mortgage life policies, especially ones offered through lenders, have a benefit that shrinks as your loan balance goes down. A level term policy keeps the same benefit for the whole term. As you pay down the mortgage, the gap between your coverage and your balance grows, and that extra goes to your family. That's one reason many people choose level coverage they own, with their family as the beneficiary.

Don't count coverage you could lose

Life insurance through work is a nice benefit, but it usually ends or changes if you leave the job, get laid off or retire. Your mortgage doesn't care where you work. Many homeowners treat work coverage as a bonus and only count coverage they own.

If two people are on the loan

If both of you earn income that helps pay the mortgage, think about coverage on both of you. Losing either paycheck could make the house hard to keep. Even a spouse who stays home adds real value, like childcare, that would cost money to replace.

What about living benefits?

Many mortgage protection plans include living benefits, which can pay part of the coverage early if you're diagnosed with a qualifying critical, chronic or terminal illness. Money paid out early usually reduces what's left for your beneficiaries. That's one more reason not to cut your coverage amount too close. What qualifies and how much can be paid depends on the carrier and the plan.

Revisit it when life changes

Check your numbers again if you refinance, move, take out a home equity loan, have a child or see a big change in income. A quick review keeps your coverage lined up with what you actually owe.
